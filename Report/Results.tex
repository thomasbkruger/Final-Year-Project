\chapter{Functional Testing}
\label{sec:functionalTesting}
This chapter details the testing which was done on the system's primary function: velocity measurement. Two distinct test setups are used, which are described in this section - results were then collected for these test setups and are presented and discussed with respect to the goals of the system and the overarching project. 

\section{C: Hard-target Speed Measurement}
\label{sec:hardTargetTesting}
\subsection{Experimental Goals}
Before attempting wind-speed measurement, where the aerosol return signal is very weak, the working principle of the system is first to be validated on a hard target, whose return signal is far stronger and whose velocity can be set precisely. This isolates set-up and processing problems from the difficulties of a weak signal, and tests performance requirements P1 (hard-target SNR),
P5 (low-speed detection) and P7 (velocity accuracy).

\subsection{Experimental Methodology}
A disc driven by a NEMA-17 stepper motor provides a constant, accurately known target speed.
The 3D-printed rig in \autoref{fig:CRig} holds the collimator at $\alpha = 45^\circ$ to the
motor axis, aimed at a point a distance $d = \SI{15}{mm}$ from the disc centre. Since the disc is at an angle to the collimator, there will be a component of its angular velocity which is in the direction (line-of-sight) of the beam. The
line-of-sight velocity at that point is
\begin{equation}
	v_{\mathrm{LOS}} = \frac{2\pi N}{60}\, d \sin\alpha ,
	\label{eq:vlos_rig}
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the motor speed in RPM. Two features of the geometry affect the measurement.
Any error in $d$ or $\alpha$ scales $v_{\mathrm{LOS}}$ directly. Additionally, the collimator output beam is \textbf{not} infinitesimally small, and thus the finite beam spot
(which is about \SI{7}{mm} in diameter \cite{f810 apc datasheet}) covers a range of radii on the disc, and hence covers a number of line-of-sight
velocities, which broadens the Doppler peak.

	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{../../../../../Downloads/fig_rig_side3d}
	\caption{Test setup used for hard-target speed measurement}
	\label{fig:CRig}
\end{figure}

The PLA disc transmits much of the light at \SI{1550}{nm}, allowing returns from stationary parts
behind it, so white paper was attached to its face to provide a strong, diffuse return. The
rest of the system was configured as in \autoref{fig:finalDesign}, with the laser at
\SI{48}{mA}. For each motor speed, %% CHECK record count
100 oscilloscope records were captured, and their Welch PSDs averaged, using the same
processing chain as experiments A and B, as detailed in \autoref(sec:design-DataAcquisition). From \autoref{eqn:DopplerSimple}, the expected Doppler shift
is \SI{1.29}{MHz} per \si{m/s} of $v_{\mathrm{LOS}}$.

%As shown in the figure, an angle of 45 degrees is used between the collimator and the motor axis, and the beam is aimed approximately $d = 15mm$ from the centre of the disc. The line of sight velocity of the beam is thus: $v_{los}=sin(45)2\pi N \times d$, where $N$ is the RPM of the motor, and $d$ is the radius from the centre of the disc to the beam spot. While care was taken in the design to precisely align the collimator with the intended point at $d=15mm$, any error in this alignment will translate directly into a measured velocity which is different from the calculated velocity. Furthermore, the beam spot has a finite size which has a diameter of approximately 7mm (from the F810-APC datasheet \cite{f810 apc datasheet}) - thus the measurement will also have contributions from other points on the disc radius which move at a different angular velocity to the intended target point. These contributions are likely to appear as a broadening of the Doppler peak.

The same data acquisition and processing chain as for experiments A and B are used here, with 100 oscilloscope records being obtained for a given motor RPM, their Welch PSDs computed, and averaged to obtain a result. From \autoref{eqn:Doppler}, substituting $\lambda = 1550nm$, it can be obtained that the velocity can be calculated from the Doppler shift, which gives a beat signal frequency of $1.29MHz$ expected per $m/s$ velocity. 

\subsection{Results and Discussion}
\autoref{fig:CPSD} below shows the PSD of the measurements obtained for different motor RPMs tested. \autoref{tab:doppler_vs_rpm} gives the Doppler peak and local noise floor for each target speed. The Doppler peak, $f_D$, is taken as the power-weighted centroid of the excess PSD at each peak, as compared to the stationary-target reference, and $v_{\mathrm{LOS}} = f_D / (\SI{1.29}{MHz.s/m})$. The noise statistics are taken in a band outside $3\times$FWHM around the centroid, out to \SI{2}{MHz} either side. All PSD values are in the same units, so the PSD at $f_D$ can be compared directly with the detection threshold (mean $+\,5\sigma$). The excess power created at the Doppler frequency is calculated by integrating each peak across the frequencies which it spans.

A distinct Doppler peak was obtained at every speed, between \SIlist{16.9;25.9}{dB} above
the local noise floor and well clear of the mean $+\,5\sigma$ detection threshold in every
case, satisfying P1. The peak height falls and its width grows with speed because the
velocity spread across the beam spot scales with $N$. However, the integrated excess power
stays constant at $(3.6 \pm 0.1)\times10^{-5}\,\si{V^2}$, which shows that the returned
power is unchanged and is merely spread over a wider band. At \SI{50}{RPM}
($v_{\mathrm{LOS}} = \SI{0.056}{m/s}$) the peak is still clearly resolved, satisfying P5;
here however, the \SI{10}{kHz} bin width of the Welch PSD (corresponding to \SI{7.75}{mm/s} of velocity) and the proximity to DC limit the precision,
which explains why this point does not show the systematic error seen at higher speeds, as discussed below.

It can also be seen that $f_{D}$, the centroid of each Doppler peak, and its corresponding measured velocity, $v_{LOS} = \frac{f_D}{1.29MHz}$, was higher than the predicted Doppler peak, by approximately 10\% for each case, causing the system to fail performance requirement P7 for the hard-target case. This illustrate visually in \autoref{fig:CentroidVSRPM}:

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{../../../../../Downloads/fig_doppler_vs_rpm}
	\caption{C: Measured Doppler peak frequency (from centroid) and expected Doppler peak frequency vs target motor RPM}
	\label{fig:CentroidVSRPM}
\end{figure}

As \autoref{fig:CentroidVSRPM} shows, $f_D$ is linear in $N$: a fit through the origin gives
\SI{1585 \pm 2}{Hz/RPM}, and all points from \SI{250}{RPM} upwards lie within \SI{0.5}{\percent}
of it. However, the slope is \SI{10.6}{\percent} above the expected \SI{1433}{Hz/RPM}, so P7 is
not met as built. Because the error is a constant scale factor, it points to the geometry rather
than to the measurement itself. Since $v_{\mathrm{LOS}}$ is proportional to $d$, the beam striking
the disc only \SI{1.6}{mm} further from the axis than intended would account for it. Over the
\SI{100}{mm} collimator-to-disc distance, that is a collimator tilt of only about \ang{0.9}.
Adjusting the collimator bracket screw changed the percentage error between experiment sets,
%% CHECK give the range observed, e.g. "from X to Y %"
while it remained constant within each set, which supports this explanation.

In summary, the experiment validates the Doppler measurement principle: the system resolves
speeds from \SIrange{0.06}{2.5}{m/s}, with a linear response and a large SNR margin on a hard
target. The \SI{10.6}{\percent} offset is a calibration error in the rig geometry, which a
single-point calibration would remove. The aerosol return in experiment D is expected to be
far weaker, so this margin does not by itself guarantee wind-speed measurement, which is
addressed in \autoref{sec:windTesting}.




\section{D: Wind-speed Measurement}
\label{sec:windTesting}
\subsection{Experimental Goals}
Now that the noise analysis of the system is complete, and the speed-measurement principle has been shown to be functional for a hard-target, the system can finally be tested for seeded air wind-speed measurement. The primary subject of investigation for this section is the sensitivity of the system with respect to its noise and the different levels of seeding in the air, as well as its accuracy at different tested speeds. As discussed previously, the range of the system can not be tested in a wind-tunnel environment, and thus this is out of scope for this project. 
\subsection{Experimental Methodology}
In order to test wind-speed measurements in a controlled environment, a test-setup was built in an existing wind-tunnel. The wind-tunnel is capable of producing measured, stable flows at 3.5 - 20m/s, with the flow-speed being measured at the centre of the test section. The tunnel has an inlet, where an air seeding device can be placed to seed the tunnel air. Two types of seeding are investigated; "fog" machines, which use mixtures of glycol or glycerin, and distilled water to produce a dense, visible vapour cloud in the air (of the same type typically seen in entertainment venues), and "misters", which simply use water to generate a very fine (barely visible) mist in the air, which is akin to atmospheric conditions on a misty day. It must be noted that at higher tunnel wind-speeds, the atmospheric seeding becomes more diffuse, and it is thus expected that for the same amount of seeding the Doppler return will decrease at higher speeds.

A small wedge which fits the collimator and which can be attached to the floor of the wind-tunnel was designed and 3D printed. The collimator is pointed in the direction of the flow, so as to minimise any residue buildup from seeded air hitting the collimator lens from front-on. The consequence of this however, is that the collimator has to be placed at an angle (35 degrees in this design), rather than straight in the direction of flow, so as to avoid the collimator and the housing from disturbing the flow and causing excessive turbulence, immediately prior to the measurement line-of-sight. This would indeed not as heavily be the case if the collimator was pointed \textbf{opposing} the flow, as measurements would then be taken in a line-of-sight \textbf{before} the housing. The angled wedge however also provides the advantage of allowing the beam to terminate on a stationary angled surface (the MDF ceiling of the wind-tunnel), rather than continuing on through the tunnel to hit the rotor which generates the flow, which would generate additional Doppler components at the radial velocity of the rotor-blades in the direction of the beam. The angled beam termination surface also serves to absorb and reflect unwanted stationary (non-Doppler shifted) beam components away from the system, so that there is minimal additional PIIN - this mimics the setup of \autoref{sec:PIINTesting}.

 It must be noted from the rig geometry, that the system only measures the component of the velocity which is in the direction of the beam. Assuming no vertical flow in the tunnel, the velocity "seen" by the beam is:
 \begin{equation}
 	 v_{los}=cos(35)\times U
 \end{equation}
 Where $U$ is the horizontal flow-speed in the wind-tunnel, as measured by its sensor. As previously derived, a 1550nm beam will be Doppler shifted by approximately $1.29MHz$ per $m/s$ of flow in the direction of the beam. Thus, for this test setup, a Doppler shift of
 \begin{equation}
 	\Delta F = 1.29MHz \times cos(35) \times U = 1.057MHz \times U
 \end{equation} 
 is expected, i.e. $1.057MHz$ per $m/s$ of wind-tunnel flow. 
 
 Again, the system is fully set up for these experiments as for C, as shown in \autoref{fig:finalDesign} and with a laser drive current of 48mA. The Welch method for calculating PSD was again used here, as for experiments A, B, and C, however in this case 500 oscilloscope records are obtained, and each segment length is set to 2500, giving a total of approximately 7200 independent averages (see \autoref{sec:design-DataAcquisition}). This gives a lower noise variance which will make the detection of a smaller Doppler peak easier, in the absence of a laser power amplifier which would increase the aerosol return, and thus increase the size of the Doppler peak.
 
 Even with this high amount of averages, the 500 records of \num{25000} samples at \SI{125}{MSa/s} represent only \SI{100}{ms} of sampled data, which is comparable to the \SIrange{4}{41}{ms} integration times of established CW lidars\cite{hill_remote_nodate}\cite{bennett_doppler_2003}. Each \SI{200}{\micro s} record is short enough that the flow is effectively frozen within it, so with a continuously streaming digitiser, with an FPGA (or similar) to process the data in real-time, the same averaging would give a new velocity estimate every \SI{0.1}{s}. This \SI{10}{Hz} update rate is well above the \SIrange{0.5}{1}{Hz} found to be
 sufficient for lidar-assisted wind turbine control \cite{letizia_characterization_2023} \cite{simley_optimizing_2018}, though below the \SI{50}{Hz} of the commercial ZephIR \cite{hill_remote_nodate}. In
 this set-up, however, the oscilloscope's transfer bottleneck spreads the records over roughly \SI{35}{s}, so each PSD is an average of the flow over that period. This is acceptable here because the tunnel speed is held constant during each capture, but in a field-deployed iteration of the system an FPGA based acquisition chain should certainly be investigated.

It can be seen that a distinct Doppler peak was obtained for every wind-tunnel setpoint, which has a power above the noise floor's mean $+ 5\sigma$ threshold, thus satisfying performance requirement P6. The peaks were significantly lower than for the hard-target case, as expected, ranging from only 0.59 - 1.96dB above the mean of the noise. It can also be seen that the Doppler peak power decreases with increased wind-tunnel speed - this is because the increased wind-speed makes the seeding in the air more diffuse, resulting in lower returned power, and thus giving a smaller distinguishable peak. Indeed, integrating the excess power of each peak (shown in the last column of the table) supports this explanation, as the excess power decreases with increased wind-speed. 

It can be seen that each peak corresponds to a line-of-sight velocity, $v_{LOS}$, which is consistently 8.1 - 11.6\% higher than the expected line-of-sight velocity at that wind-tunnel speed setpoint. The wind-tunnel's wind-speed sensor was compared against an accurate pitot-tube anemometer in order to verify that the tunnel's setpoints are accurate, and it was found that this was the case, thus ruling setpoint error out as a possible cause of the DWL system's approximate 10\% overshoot in all measurements.
