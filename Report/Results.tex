\chapter{Functional Testing}
\label{sec:functionalTesting}
This chapter details the testing which was done on the system's primary function: velocity measurement. Two distinct test setups are used, which are described in this section - results were then collected for these test setups and are presented and discussed with respect to the goals of the system and the overarching project. 

\section{C: Hard-target Speed Measurement}
\label{sec:hardTargetTesting}
\subsection{Experimental Goals}
Before attempting wind-speed measurement, where the aerosol return signal is very weak, the working principle of the system is first to be validated on a hard target, whose return signal is far stronger and whose velocity can be set precisely. This isolates set-up and processing problems from the difficulties of a weak signal, and tests performance requirements P4 (hard-target SNR),
P5 (low-speed detection) and P7 (velocity accuracy).

\subsection{Experimental Methodology}
A disc driven by a NEMA-17 stepper motor provides a constant, accurately known target speed.
The 3D-printed rig in \autoref{fig:CRig} holds the collimator at $\alpha = 45^\circ$ to the
motor axis, aimed at a point a distance $d = \SI{15}{mm}$ from the disc centre. Since the disc is at an angle to the collimator, there will be a component of its angular velocity which is in the direction (line-of-sight) of the beam. The
line-of-sight velocity at that point is
\begin{equation}
	v_{\mathrm{LOS}} = \frac{2\pi N}{60}\, d \sin\alpha ,
	\label{eq:vlos_rig}
\end{equation}
where $N$ is the motor speed in RPM. Two features of the geometry affect the measurement.
Any error in $d$ or $\alpha$ scales $v_{\mathrm{LOS}}$ directly. Additionally, the collimator output beam is \textbf{not} infinitesimally small, and thus the finite beam spot
(which is about \SI{7}{mm} in diameter) covers a range of radii on the disc, and hence covers a number of line-of-sight
velocities, which broadens the Doppler peak.

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.7\linewidth]{"../Data Acquisition/figures/fig_rig_side3d"}
	\caption{Test setup used for hard-target speed measurement}
	\label{fig:CRig}
\end{figure}

The PLA disc transmits much of the light at \SI{1550}{nm}, allowing returns from stationary parts
behind it, so white paper was attached to its face to provide a strong, diffuse return. The
rest of the system was configured as in \autoref{fig:finalDesign}, with the laser at
\SI{48}{mA}. For each motor speed, %% CHECK record count
100 oscilloscope records were captured, and their Welch PSDs averaged, using the same
processing chain as experiments A and B, as detailed in \autoref{sec:design-DataAcquisition}. From \autoref{eqn:DopplerSimple}, the expected Doppler shift
is \SI{1.29}{MHz} per \si{m/s} of $v_{\mathrm{LOS}}$.

\subsection{Results and Discussion}
\autoref{fig:CPSD} below shows the PSD of the measurements obtained for different motor RPMs tested. \autoref{tab:doppler_vs_rpm} gives the Doppler peak and local noise floor for each target speed. The Doppler peak, $f_D$, is taken as the power-weighted centroid of the excess PSD at each peak, as compared to the stationary-target reference, and $v_{\mathrm{LOS}} = f_D / (\SI{1.29}{MHz.s/m})$. The noise statistics are taken in a band outside $3\times$FWHM around the centroid, out to \SI{2}{MHz} either side. All PSD values are in the same units, so the PSD at $f_D$ can be compared directly with the detection threshold (mean $+\,5\sigma$). The excess power created at the Doppler frequency is calculated by integrating each peak across the frequencies which it spans. This integrated excess is the mean-square voltage of the beat signal, which Equation~\ref{eq:beat_power} relates directly to the received optical power.

A distinct Doppler peak was obtained at every speed, between \SIlist{16.9;25.9}{dB} above
the local noise floor and well clear of the mean $+\,5\sigma$ detection threshold in every
case, satisfying P4. The peak height falls and its width grows with speed because the
velocity spread across the beam spot scales with $N$. However, the integrated excess power
stays constant at $(3.6 \pm 0.1)\times10^{-5}\,\si{V^2}$, which shows that the returned
power is unchanged and is merely spread over a wider band. At \SI{50}{RPM}
($v_{\mathrm{LOS}} = \SI{0.056}{m/s}$) the peak is still clearly resolved, satisfying P5;
here however, the \SI{10}{kHz} bin width of the Welch PSD (corresponding to \SI{7.75}{mm/s} of velocity) and the proximity to DC limit the precision,
which explains why this point does not show the systematic error seen at higher speeds, as discussed below.

It can be seen that $f_{D}$, the centroid of each Doppler peak, and its corresponding measured velocity, $v_{LOS} = \frac{f_D}{1.29MHz}$, was higher than the predicted Doppler peak, by approximately 10\% for each case, causing the system to fail performance requirement P7 for the hard-target case. This is illustrated visually in \autoref{fig:CentroidVSRPM}:

\begin{figure}[H]
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{"../Data Acquisition/figures/fig_doppler_vs_rpm"}
	\caption{C: Measured Doppler peak frequency (from centroid) and expected Doppler peak frequency vs target motor RPM}
	\label{fig:CentroidVSRPM}
\end{figure}

As \autoref{fig:CentroidVSRPM} shows, $f_D$ is linear in $N$: a fit through the origin gives
\SI{1585 \pm 2}{Hz/RPM}, and all points from \SI{250}{RPM} upwards lie within \SI{0.5}{\percent}
of it. However, the slope is \SI{10.6}{\percent} above the expected \SI{1433}{Hz/RPM}, so P7 is
not met as built. Because the error is a constant scale factor, it points to the geometry rather
than to the measurement itself. Since $v_{\mathrm{LOS}}$ is proportional to $d$, the beam striking
the disc only \SI{1.6}{mm} further from the axis than intended would account for it. Over the
\SI{100}{mm} collimator-to-disc distance, that is a collimator tilt of only about \ang{0.9}.
Adjusting the collimator bracket screw changed the percentage error between experiment sets,
%% CHECK give the range observed, e.g. "from X to Y %"
while it remained constant within each set, which supports this explanation.

In summary, the experiment validates the Doppler measurement principle: the system resolves
speeds from \SIrange{0.06}{2.5}{m/s}, with a linear response and a large SNR margin on a hard
target, thus meeting P4 and the low-end velocity detection range of P5. The \SI{10.6}{\percent} velocity offset is a calibration error in the rig geometry, which a
single-point calibration would remove. The aerosol return in experiment D is expected to be
far weaker, so this margin does not by itself guarantee wind-speed measurement, which is
addressed in \autoref{sec:windTesting}.




\section{D: Wind-speed Measurement}
\label{sec:windTesting}
\subsection{Experimental Goals}
Now that the noise analysis of the system is complete, and the speed-measurement principle has been shown to be functional for a hard-target, the system can finally be tested for seeded air wind-speed measurement. The primary subject of investigation for this section is the sensitivity of the system with respect to its noise and the different levels of seeding in the air, as well as its accuracy at different tested speeds. As discussed previously, the range of the system can not be tested in a wind-tunnel environment, and thus this is out of scope for this project. 
\subsection{Experimental Methodology}
In order to test wind-speed measurements in a controlled environment, a test-setup was built in an existing wind-tunnel. The wind-tunnel is capable of producing measured, stable flows at 3 - 21m/s, at the centre of the test section. The tunnel has an inlet, where an air seeding device can be placed to seed the tunnel air. Two types of seeding are investigated; "fog" machines, which use mixtures of glycol or glycerin, and distilled water to produce a dense, visible vapour cloud in the air (of the same type typically seen in entertainment venues), and "misters", which simply use water to generate a very fine (barely visible) mist in the air.

A small wedge which fits the collimator and which can be attached to the floor of the wind-tunnel was designed and 3D printed. The collimator is pointed downstream of the flow, so as to minimise any residue buildup from seeded air hitting the collimator lens from front-on. The consequence of this however, is that the collimator has to be placed at an angle (35 degrees in this design), rather than straight in the direction of flow, so as to avoid the collimator and the housing from disturbing the flow and causing excessive turbulence, immediately prior to the measurement line-of-sight. The angled wedge also provides the advantage of allowing the beam to terminate on a stationary angled surface (the MDF ceiling of the wind-tunnel), rather than continuing on through the tunnel to hit the rotor which generates the flow, which would generate additional Doppler components at the radial velocity of the rotor-blades in the direction of the beam. The angled beam termination surface also serves to absorb and reflect unwanted stationary (non-Doppler shifted) beam components away from the system, so that there is minimal additional PIIN - this mimics the setup of \autoref{sec:PIINTesting}.

 It must be noted from the rig geometry, that the system only measures the component of the velocity which is in the direction of the beam. Assuming no vertical flow in the tunnel, the line-of-sight velocity "seen" by the beam is:
 \begin{equation}
 	 v_{los}=cos(35)\times U
 \end{equation}
 Where $U$ is the horizontal flow-speed in the wind-tunnel, as measured by its sensor. As previously calculated, a 1550nm beam will be Doppler shifted by approximately $1.29MHz$ per $m/s$ of flow in the direction of the beam. Thus, for this test setup, a Doppler shift of
 \begin{equation}
 	\Delta F = 1.29MHz \times cos(35) \times U = 1.057MHz \times U
 \end{equation} 
 is expected, i.e. $1.057MHz$ per $m/s$ of wind-tunnel flow. 
 
 Again, the system is fully set up for these experiments as for C, as shown in \autoref{fig:finalDesign} and with a laser drive current of 48mA. The Welch method for calculating PSD was again used here, as for experiments A, B, and C, however in this case 500 oscilloscope records are obtained, and each segment length is set to 2500, giving a total of approximately 7200 independent averages (see \autoref{sec:design-DataAcquisition}). This gives a lower noise variance which will make the detection of a smaller Doppler peak easier, in the absence of a laser power amplifier which would increase the aerosol return, and thus increase the size of the Doppler peak.
 
 Even with this high amount of averages, the 500 records of \num{25000} samples at \SI{125}{MSa/s} represent only \SI{100}{ms} of sampled data, which is comparable to the \SIrange{4}{41}{ms} integration times of established CW lidars\cite{hill_remote_nodate}\cite{bennett_doppler_2003}. Each \SI{200}{\micro s} record is short enough that the flow is effectively frozen within it, so with a continuously streaming digitiser, with an FPGA (or similar) to process the data in real-time, the same averaging would give a new velocity estimate every \SI{0.1}{s}. This \SI{10}{Hz} update rate is well above the \SIrange{0.5}{1}{Hz} found to be
 sufficient for lidar-assisted wind turbine control \cite{simley_optimizing_2018}, though below the \SI{50}{Hz} of the commercial ZephIR \cite{hill_remote_nodate}. In
 this set-up, however, the oscilloscope's transfer bottleneck spreads the records over roughly \SI{60}{s}, so each PSD is an average of the flow over that period. This is acceptable here because the tunnel speed is held constant during each capture, but in a field-deployed iteration of the system an FPGA based acquisition chain should certainly be investigated.

It can be seen that at every speed tested in dense fog, a clear Doppler peak was detected above the mean $+5\sigma$ threshold, with a test setup which gives less than 10 000 averages, thus fulfilling performance requirement P6. It can also be seen that at higher speeds, the excess power at the Doppler peak decreases; this is because the seeding becomes more diffuse in the air at higher speeds, thus making the returned signal smaller, and making the peak clear the detection threshold with less margin. 

After measuring the system's sensitivity performance in the dense-fog seeded air, the system was tested using the same test setup, but this time using very light mist seeding, which is more akin to real-world atmospheric conditions. Since the significantly lighter seeding gives a smaller return signal, particularly in faster wind-tunnel speeds, more than one capture is obtained at each speed, so that more averages can be taken. A capture here is defined as 500 oscilloscope records, as discussed in the methodology. It can be seen in \autoref{tab:D_mist_avg} that at \SI{5}{m/s} the light mist
gives a substantially weaker signal return than dense fog at the same tunnel speed:
its excess power is approximately half the \num{8.87e-7}\,\si{V^2} measured in
fog (\autoref{tab:D_snr}), a reduction of about \SIrange[range-phrase=--,
range-units=single]{3}{4}{dB}. As a result, with a single 500-record capture
(about \num{7200} independent averages, see \autoref{sec:design-DataAcquisition}) the peak lies below the mean $+\,5\sigma$ detection threshold. With two captures it becomes visible in \autoref{fig:psd5mpsmistaveraging}, but its value at the centroid (\SI{0.114}{dB}) still falls just short of the
\SI{0.131}{dB} threshold, making this a marginal case. With three captures
(about \num{21600} independent averages) the peak clears the threshold
unambiguously. \autoref{fig:psd5mpsmistaveraging} shows that averaging does
not raise the Doppler peak, which remains at approximately \SI{0.14}{dB} (its
variation between captures is within the noise of a single bin), but reduces
the fluctuation of the noise floor, lowering the threshold from
\SI{0.255}{dB} to \SI{0.116}{dB} and so increasing the size of the peak
\textbf{relative} to the noise. This illustrates how averaging increases the
sensitivity of a system that, lacking an optical power amplifier
(\autoref{sec:design-ComponentChoices}), has a weak aerosol return.

%The narrow spur near \SI{12.5}{MHz} exceeds the threshold line in every panel, but it is not a Doppler return: its frequency does not change with tunnel speed or averaging, it is only a few bins wide compared with the \SIrange[range-phrase=--, range-units=single]{0.75}{1.0}{MHz} width of the aerosol peak. It is expected that this spur comes from a noise source in the data acquisition chain. For this test, the $5\sigma$ threshold is evaluated only within the local noise band around $f_D$, and it guards against random noise giving false detection. However, in a real world system where it is not known where the peak is anticipated, spurs such as this must instead be identified from a reference spectrum when no wind-speed is present, so as to avoid false detection of particular wind-speeds. This problem will indeed however be less prevalent if a power amplifier is used to give larger detectable peaks, with respect to spurious peaks like this one. 

Although the light-mist case does not meet P6 within 10\,000 averages, it
illustrates both the benefit and the limit of averaging. At 5\,m/s the Doppler
peak lies only $\approx$0.14\,dB (3.3\,\%) above the noise floor, whereas the
mean\,+\,$5\sigma$ threshold at $N = 10\,000$ is 5\,\% above it. A further $\approx$2\,dB of
signal-to-noise ratio is therefore needed to meet P6, consistent with the
$\approx$23\,000 averages found necessary in practice. However, because
$\sigma \propto 1/\sqrt{N}$ (see \autoref{sec:design-DataAcquisition}), each doubling of $N$ gains only 1.5\,dB, so
averaging cannot practically close larger gaps, and measurement in unseeded, clean air is such a gap:

The current laser power emitted is estimated at approximately 15mW - from the 17mW at the diode output for a current of 48mA (see result of experiment A2), only 90\% is directed at the atmospheric emission, and power loss in the fiber is ignored here. Converting the integrated excess power of the 5\,m/s peak to optical power
using Equation~\ref{eq:beat_power} gives an effective received power here of
roughly 0.1\,pW, from the 15mW emitted power. In open air, where the beam is not cut short by the tunnel
ceiling and \autoref{eqn:backscatterPower} applies, clear air
($\beta(\pi) \approx 10^{-8}$\,m$^{-1}$sr$^{-1}$ \cite{hill_remote_nodate}) would return only
$\approx$0.7\,fW from the transmitted beam by this system in its current form, which is 22--25\,dB lower than the light mist-seeded case. A clean-air measurement would therefore require roughly 25--30\,dB of signal increase or noise reduction, equivalent to about $10^5$ times more averaging. This is approximately what an
erbium-doped fibre amplifier (see discussion on laser power level in \autoref{sec:design-ComponentChoices}) raising the transmit power to 1\,W (+18\,dB above 15mW),
combined with removing the residual PIIN to reach the shot-noise limit
(+5.7\,dB, \autoref{sec:PIINTesting}), would provide. Thus, these measurements in seeded air give a clear upgrade-path to the system, and are able to quantify how much more sensitivity is required in the system in order to measure wind-speed in unseeded air. It also illustrates that simply taking more averages to increase the sensitivity is not viable to achieve wind-speed detection in unseeded air.

It was found that using the original test setup detailed in the methodology for this section (downstream facing collimator and wedge), an average overshoot of approximately 10.7\% was consistently observed between the DWL measured velocity (as calculated from the peak's frequency), and the expected line-of-sight velocity, as calculated from the wind-tunnel's velocity setpoint. However, the data showed very good linearity, with $R^2=0.9998$, thus the overshoot offset can easily be removed via a single-point calibration, to give an error of approximately 1.1\%. Without this calibration, the system fails performance requirement P7 on velocity accuracy within $5\%$, but after the calibration it passes. Thus, it is important to investigate whether the calibration, which assumes error from the test setup, is valid.

% It can also be seen that the Doppler peak had a width of approximately 0.45 - 0.65MHz at its half-maximum value, corresponding to a velocity range of 0.35 - 0.5m/s across the beam's volume - this is expected, given that the Doppler peak represents the equally-weighted Doppler shift from velocity components along the entire beam (rather than just at the center), since no focusing is used (see \autoref{sec:lit-Ranging} and \autoref{sec:design-ComponentChoices}), and naturally the wind-speed will vary across the tunnel's area and give varying Doppler shifts near the setpoint.

The cause of the 10.7\% overshoot was therefore further investigated: The laser's frequency is stable at the expected 1550nm (a drift causing 10\% error would require a drifted wavelength of 1400nm, which is not possible for a DFB diode), the data acquisition chain was validated against a signal generated by a signal generator, and the wind-tunnel's setpoint speeds were validated against a calibrated anemometer. Thus it is hypothesised that this error is due to the turbulence and blockage caused by the test-rig geometry, which may redirect a significant component of the flow over the top of the wedge, and thus into the line of sight of the collimator, so as to cause an overshoot in its measurement relative to the wind-tunnel setpoint. The test setup was modified to investigate this, so as to have the wedge and collimator now pointing upstream of the wind. The results of this setup are plotted in \autoref{fig:figvelocityboth} alongside the "expected" measurement from the wind-tunnel setpoint, and the results from the original downstream setup. It can be seen that with this new setup, the overshoot was reduced to an average of 6.9\%, while retaining the linearity of the original setup. It is thus clear that at least some component of the overshoot is due to the test-rig geometry, and that it is thus valid to remove the offset using a single-point calibration. Rigorously profiling the tunnel flow for a given test rig would however be needed to test this hypothesis, and the velocity accuracy further (see recommendation R4).

\subsubsection{Summary of results}
In dense fog, a Doppler peak was detected above the mean\,$+\,5\sigma$
threshold at every tunnel speed from \SIrange[range-phrase=--,
range-units=single]{3.1}{21}{m/s} within \num{10000} averages, meeting sensitivity requirement P6. These wind-tunnel speeds correspond to beam line-of-sight speeds of \SIrange[range-phrase=--,
range-units=single]{2.54}{17.2}{m/s}, and thus the peak at the highest tunnel speeds demonstrates the system's high-end velocity detection above 15m/s (LOS), meeting P5. In
light mist at a tunnel setpoint of \SI{5}{m/s}, where the return was approximately half that in
fog, detection required approximately \num{21600} averages, so P6 is not met
in this case.

 The measured velocity was highly linear with tunnel speed
($R^2 = 0.9998$) but overshot by \SI{10.7}{\percent}, failing P7 as built. A
single-factor calibration reduces the error to \SI{1.1}{\percent}, which causes P7 to finally be met.
The overshoot is at least partly attributable to the test-rig geometry, and
the calibration is therefore specific to the installation. Finally,
measurement in clean air is estimated to require a further
\SIrange[range-phrase=--, range-units=single]{25}{30}{dB} of sensitivity,
dominated by transmit power and residual PIIN, which averaging alone cannot
practically provide.
