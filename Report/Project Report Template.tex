{\Large Abstract}\\
\hrule

Characterisation of the wind field around wind turbines is an important objective in the wind-energy sector. Current methods which use anemometers mounted on met-masts are limited to single points in space, can be costly to erect, and suffer from the effects of the mast’s wake on measurements. LiDAR systems mounted on wind-turbine nacelles present a remote-sensing alternative which allows for wind-speed measurement from a distance, thus providing greater spatial coverage. Doppler wind LiDAR (DWL) systems emit a beam which is backscattered by aerosols and small particles, and measure the Doppler shift of the backscatter, from which the speed of the particles, and hence the wind speed, is determined. Due to inherent challenges in measuring the small backscattered signal, these instruments are complex and require much design consideration, and for smaller research groups, scientific access is limited by the high cost of quote-only commercial systems.

In this study, an all-fibre, continuous-wave, homodyne coherent DWL system with balanced detection is designed, built and tested, with constraints on component cost and availability, particularly in the absence of an optical power amplifier. The study demonstrates the principles involved in these systems, and makes performance (primarily in terms of sensitivity) attributable to component and architecture choice. Of particular importance in the investigation is the shot-noise limit, which is the quantum limit on the system’s noise, and therefore sets the best sensitivity a coherent system can achieve. The detector is shown to operate within 0.7 dB of this limit; however, phase-induced intensity noise arising from spurious reflections in the fibre paths dominates in the full system, initially raising the noise floor 22 dB above the shot-noise limit. Matching the fibre path lengths of the local oscillator and return signal reduces this to approximately 5.7 dB, though this is unstable between measurements. With this, the system is found capable of measuring wind speed at all tested flow rates (3–21 m/s) in air seeded with dense fog to account for the lower emitted optical power, with a 10.7\% overshoot before calibration, attributed in part to the test rig, and an error of 1.1\% after it. Recommendations are made for potential improvements of the system, particularly the addition of an optical power amplifier, a narrower-linewidth laser source, focusing optics and real-time processing.
