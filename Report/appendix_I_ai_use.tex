\subsubsection{Response}

\begin{tcolorbox}[breakable, colback=gray!4, colframe=gray!50,
	boxrule=0.4pt, arc=2pt, fontupper=\small,
	left=6pt, right=6pt, top=4pt, bottom=4pt]
	Let me check what Harris et al.\ and Jiang et al.\ say about the spectral shape before answering.
	
	\textit{[Searches the Harris et al.\ (1998) paper for its discussion of delay, coherence and spurious reflections, and reads Section~5.]}
	
	Yes. Harris et al.\ (1998), your coherence-length paper, covers this almost exactly in its Section~5, ``Excess local oscillator noise''. Jiang et al.\ (2019) supports it. The roll-off doesn't come from the laser linewidth. It comes from the \textbf{delay between the LO and the reflected light}, and its frequency tells you how long that delay is.
	
	\subsubsection*{What Harris says}
	\begin{itemize}
		\item In a monostatic fibre lidar, weak reflections ``from within the coupler and from fibre connectors'' interfere with the LO. Because they travel different distances, ``this gives rise to delayed self-heterodyne spectra'', and the noise floor ``has essentially the same origin as the incoherent component in the delayed self-heterodyne spectra'' of their Section~3.
		\item The broadband part of that spectrum has ``ripples\ldots{} separated by the inverse of the delay time'', and ``the relative distances can be inferred from the shape of the spectra''. In other words, the shape of the spectrum encodes the path mismatch.
		\item For delays much shorter than the coherence length, the low-frequency excess noise scales with the \textbf{square of the fibre-path mismatch} and inversely with the coherence length (their Eqs.~3--5). Adding 4\,m of fibre made the excess noise rise ``dramatically''.
		\item They saw excess LO noise 5--30\,dB above shot noise that was ``extremely unstable and very sensitive to small disturbances of the optical fibres''. That describes your 4-capture spread.
	\end{itemize}
	
	Jiang et al.\ add the observation that PIIN is strongest near zero frequency and can sit orders of magnitude above shot noise. That is qualitatively what your plot shows: a flat region at low frequency that is well above the A2 floor.
	
	\subsubsection*{The mechanism, and why the roll-off appears where it does}
	The reflection interferes with the LO at a slowly drifting static phase $\varphi_0$. The laser's phase noise over the delay $\tau$, $\Delta\varphi(t) = \varphi(t) - \varphi(t-\tau)$, turns into intensity noise. For a laser with a Lorentzian linewidth $\Delta\nu$ and $\tau$ much shorter than the coherence time:
	\[
	S_{\mathrm{PIIN}}(f) \propto P_{\mathrm{LO}}\,P_r\,\sin^2\!\varphi_0\,\Delta\nu\,\frac{\sin^2(\pi f\tau)}{f^2}
	= S_{\mathrm{PIIN}}(0)\,\mathrm{sinc}^2(f\tau),
	\qquad S_{\mathrm{PIIN}}(0) \propto \Delta\nu\,\tau^2
	\]
	\begin{itemize}
		\item It is \textbf{flat} at low frequencies, starts to fall above about $1/(4\tau)$, is \textbf{3\,dB down at about $0.44/\tau$}, and has its first null at $\mathbf{1/\tau}$. The nulls are Harris's ``ripples''.
		\item The $\Delta\nu\,\tau^2$ factor is Harris's ``$\propto$ (fibre length)$^2$ / coherence length''.
		\item It is \textbf{not} a Lorentzian with a 2\,MHz width. That shape only applies when $\tau$ is much longer than the coherence time, and here $\tau_c = 1/(\pi\Delta\nu) \approx 160$\,ns, or about 33\,m of fibre. Your flat region extends well past 2\,MHz, which confirms you're in the short-delay regime Harris describes.
	\end{itemize}
	
	\subsubsection*{What your curve implies}
	I read your plot approximately. I removed the A2 floor in power and normalised by the A2 shot-noise excess, which is white at the diode, so dividing by it cancels the detector's gain peaking and the scope's roll-off. The PIIN then falls by about $-2.3$\,dB at 20\,MHz, $-6.6$\,dB at 40\,MHz and $-12$\,dB at 60\,MHz. A sinc$^2$ fit to those points gives $\boldsymbol{\tau \approx 13\text{--}20}$\,\textbf{ns}, which is a path mismatch of about \textbf{3--4\,m of fibre} (at 0.204\,m per ns). That's plausible given the circulator pigtails, the collimator patch cord and the coupler leads. The spread in the fitted $\tau$ across frequency suggests that several reflections with different delays are contributing, which Harris also describes.
	
	This gives you a good check for the report. Add up the fibre lengths from the splitter to the coupler along the LO path, and along the path through the circulator to the collimator and back. Their difference should give $\tau$, and its sinc$^2$ prediction should match the measured roll-off. Worth remembering: the 10\,cm of free space to your matte surface adds only about 0.7\,ns, so the delay is dominated by fibre.
	
	\subsubsection*{Why the 4 captures differ so much}
	The $\sin^2\varphi_0$ term explains it. The interferometric phase between the LO and the reflection wanders with temperature and with fibre movement. Near quadrature ($\varphi_0 = 90^\circ$) PIIN is at its maximum; near $\varphi_0 = 0$ it almost vanishes. Your min--max band is about $2\times$ in voltage (6\,dB in power) at low frequency and keeps the same shape across frequency. That is what a phase factor would do: change the amplitude and leave the shape alone. (Harris's lidar used an 80\,MHz frequency offset, which averages this phase out; your homodyne system doesn't, which is why yours fluctuates.)
	
	For the analysis:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item \textbf{Four captures are too few} to average over a random $\sin^2\varphi_0$. Take 20--50 captures spread over several minutes. Report the \textbf{mean} (which tends to half the maximum) as typical performance, and the \textbf{maximum} as the worst case (quadrature).
		\item \textbf{Fit the shape rather than the level.} Normalise each capture by its own low-frequency value; the curves should collapse onto one sinc$^2$, and $\tau$ is the robust result.
		\item \textbf{Check which reflection dominates} with the B0--B3 breakdown from before. Note that $S_{\mathrm{PIIN}}(0)$ scales with $\tau^2$, so a reflection at the end of a long fibre path matters far more than an equally strong one close to the coupler.
	\end{itemize}
\end{tcolorbox}

 		\item \textbf{Chunked PSD:} it's computed 100 records at a time, because all 1000 at once would need about 1\,GB of RAM. The result is identical.
 		\item \textbf{No per-record PSDs saved:} at 1000 records they made the file 47\,MB, bigger than the raw capture. Without them it's 63\,kB, and they can be recomputed from the raw file.
 	\end{itemize}
 	
 	\textbf{Test results with the simulated scope}
 	\begin{itemize}
 		\item The full run (capture, save, PSD, reference overlay, plot) worked. A simulated 20\,mV rms of noise plus a 4\,mV tone came out at the expected total.
 		\item \texttt{--dry-run} saves nothing and skips the PSD, as before.
 		\item I fixed one bug the test found: \texttt{--n 50} was being read as the new script's \texttt{--nperseg} instead of reaching \texttt{Acquisition.py}.
 		\item One test run wrote two files into your real \texttt{data/} folder; I deleted them straight away. The other test files are only in the scratchpad.
 	\end{itemize}
 	
 	Each run will take about 100\,s and write about 42\,MB of raw data.
 	
 	\textbf{Check the Doppler frequency range before you start:} at 1550\,nm the shift is 1.29\,MHz per m/s along the beam. In the RPM data the detector starts rolling off around 20\,MHz, so flows above about 15\,m/s along the beam will fall into the roll-off. A beam angle to the flow lowers the shift.
