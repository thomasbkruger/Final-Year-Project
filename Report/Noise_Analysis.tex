\chapter{Noise Analysis}
\label{sec:noiseAnalysis}

 \section{Introduction}
 This chapter gives a comprehensive account of the testing which was done on the completed DWL system to account for the various sources of noise. The purpose of the noise analysis is to quantify the noise observed in the system and attribute it to the appropriate sources, while investigating how component, architecture, and operating point choice affects this. This chapter is separate from the functional testing of \autoref{sec:functionalTesting}, as the experimental setup differs significantly, and the results from these experiments will indeed be used to inform the expected results of the experiments in \autoref{sec:functionalTesting}, and to construct an optimal experimental setup in those cases.

 \section{A: Electronic and Shot Noise}
 
 \subsection{Experimental Goals}
 As discussed in \autoref{sec:lit-noiseSources}, a coherent DWL performs best when it is
 shot-noise limited: the shot noise of the local oscillator (LO) must dominate every other
 noise source at the detector. Experiment A characterises the receiver's noise floor with
 the LO as the only light on the detector, so that the individual noise contributions can be
 separated and measured. It has the following goals:
 
 \begin{itemize}
 	\item \textbf{Measure the electronic noise floor (A1).} With the laser off, measure the
 	noise of the PD100B and acquisition chain alone, and compare it with the input-referred
 	noise specified in the PD100B datasheet. Agreement with the datasheet validates the
 	data collection and post-processing chain used in all later experiments.
 	
 	\item \textbf{Measure shot noise as a function of LO power (A2).} Step the laser drive
 	current to vary the LO power reaching the balanced detector, and confirm that the excess
 	noise above the electronic floor grows linearly with LO power, as expected for shot noise.
 	
 	\item \textbf{Choose the operating point.} From the A2 results, find the LO power at which
 	shot noise overtakes the electronic floor, and choose a laser operating point at which the
 	system is close enough to the shot-noise limit to meet requirement P1
 	(\autoref{tab:requirements}).
 	
 	\item \textbf{Quantify RIN and validate balanced detection (A3).} Repeat the measurement
 	with only one photodiode connected, making the PD100B a single-ended detector. Comparing
 	this with the balanced case measures the laser's relative intensity noise (RIN) and
 	confirms that the balanced detector cancels this common-mode noise as intended. This experiment is only included for completeness, to demonstrate RIN cancellation, and the results are given in Appendix \ref{appendix:RINValidation}.
 \end{itemize}

 It can be seen that this differs from the full system setup. The circulator and collimator are unused for this experiment. The 90\% splitter arm is left unconnected and is capped. Only the 10\% splitter arm (LO) is connected to the one arm of the 50:50 coupler. Both outputs of the coupler are connected to the balanced detector, except in the case of experiment A3. Since there is no circulator, collimator or target, the only light reaching the detector is the LO itself. With no second optical field there is no beat term (as in \autoref{eqn:beatSignal}), and the LO contributes only a DC photocurrent, which is removed by the detector's AC coupling. Thus, the signal at the detector will be a pure noise signal, containing only electronic noise and shot noise. Note that phase-induced intensity noise is not present here, as it originates from reflections in the collimator and circulator which are not present in this setup, and RIN is assumed to be cancelled fully by balanced detection.

 For each measurement the laser is held at a fixed drive current and
 100 records of the detector output are captured sequentially with the oscilloscope and transferred to a laptop. The averaged PSD of the record is estimated, as described in \autoref{sec:design-DataAcquisition}. A1 requires only a single measurement, while A2 and A3 are repeated over a range of drive
 currents up to the 48\,mA operating point.
 
 To compare the results with a single figure, the PSD can be averaged over the 5--20\,MHz band.
 From \autoref{eq:DopplerShift}, this band corresponds to target speeds of roughly
 4--15.5\,m/s, making it a reasonable band to take an average noise power measurement over, as it is where the Doppler peaks in subsequent tests would appear.

\subsection{Results and Discussion}
\subsubsection{A1: Electronic Noise}
The PSD for the measurement was obtained which gives the electronic noise from the detector only. The average PSD over the 5 - 20MHz band is calculated (using the script) as:
\begin{equation}
	PSD_{avg (5 - 20MHz)} = 8.06\times 10^{-14}V^2/Hz
\end{equation}

Using the PD100B detector's gain value of 39kV/A, this can be converted into an average input noise current density across 5 - 20MHz, for comparison to the PD100B datasheet:

\begin{equation}
    I_{input} = \frac{8.06\times 10^{-14}V^2/Hz}{(39kV/A)^2}=7.28pA/\sqrt{Hz}
\end{equation}

Input noise current density can be seen as the equivalent of injecting this noise current at the detector input. The calculated average of $7.28pA/\sqrt{Hz}$ across 5 - 20MHz agrees closely with the PD100B's datasheet value of $8pA/\sqrt{Hz}$, measured at 10MHz (Appendix \ref{appendix:pd100b}). Thus, this level of electronic noise is expected, and the measurement and post processing chain is validated. 

In order to achieve shot-noise limited operation, the average PSD of the shot-noise over this region must thus be $\gg8.06\times 10^{-14}V^2/Hz$.

\subsubsection{A2: Electronic Noise \& Shot Noise}
\autoref{fig:A2PSD} gives the PSD plots of the total noise - made up of shot-noise and electronic noise - for different laser currents on the same axes:

	\label{fig:A2PSD}

It can be seen that the total noise increases with increased laser current - this is expected, due to the linear relationship between shot noise and LO power (which is related to laser current which is varied here). In order to compare the PSDs for the various laser currents, the average PSD across the 5 - 20MHz range can again be taken here, for each laser current. \autoref{fig:A2PSDvsCurrent} gives these averages plotted against laser current (table of results in Appendix \ref{appendix:A2Table}):

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\linewidth]{psd_vs_current.png}
    \caption{5 - 20MHz Average PSD vs Laser Current with Balanced Detection}
    \label{fig:A2PSDvsCurrent}
\end{figure}

It can clearly be seen in \autoref{fig:A2PSDvsCurrent} and \autoref{fig:A2PSD} that the shot-noise exceeds the electronic noise floor at even the lowest measured laser currents, thus making the system shot-noise limited. As the current is increased, the system approaches the ideal shot-noise limit, as shot noise increasingly swamps out the electronic noise floor. 

Performance requirement P1 in \autoref{tab:requirements} requires that the total detector noise is within 1dB of the shot-noise limit. This requirement can be decomposed as follows:

Noise power adds in linear units, so letting $S$ be shot noise, and $E$ be every other noise source present here. The total noise floor, $T$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
    T = S + E = S(1+\frac{E}{S})
\end{equation}

Converting to dB:

\begin{equation}
    10log_{10}(S(1+\frac{E}{S}))=10log_{10}(S) + 10log_{10}(1 + \frac{E}{S})
\end{equation}
In this form, it can clearly be seen that as $S$ increases, the effect of $E$ on the total noise floor is increasingly reduced, as the second term, $10log_{10}(1+\frac{E}{S})$ approaches zero. This second term is the excess noise above the ideal shot-noise limit, which is given by $10log_{10}(S)$. Thus, in order to obtain a total noise floor which is within 1dB of the shot noise limit, we set:

\begin{equation}
    10log_{10}(1+\frac{E}{S})=1dB
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    \xrightarrow{}\frac{E}{S}\leq0.26
    \label{eqn:A2Crossover}
\end{equation}

Substituting the obtained electronic noise floor obtained in A1 into \autoref{eqn:A2Crossover}, we obtain:

\begin{equation}
    S \geq 3.1 \times 10^{-13}V^2/Hz
\end{equation}

Noting that \autoref{fig:A2PSD} and \autoref{fig:A2PSDvsCurrent} both give the \textbf{total} detector noise floor (shot \textbf{and} electronic noise), we require the total noise to be above $3.906 \times 10^{-13}V^2/Hz$ to give a shot noise value of $3.1 \times 10^{-13}V^2/Hz$. This can be seen to be reached at a laser current of approximately 32mA. Above 32mA, the total detector noise floor is within 1dB of the ideal shot-noise limit, and the ideal shot-noise limit is further approached as the current is increased. 

From this, it can be seen that an operating current of 48mA for the laser allows P1 in \autoref{tab:requirements} to be met with the highest margin, giving a shot noise value of $4.49\times 10^{-13}V^2/Hz$, and a total combined noise of $5.29\times 10^{-13}V^2/Hz$, and this will thus be used as the laser current for the remainder of this project. While a higher laser current still may be used, to even further approach the ideal shot-noise limit, this risks saturating the detector. The detector has a maximum incident power rating of 1.5mW on each photodiode (Appendix \ref{appendix:pd100b}). From the laser datasheet (Appendix \ref{appendix:laser}), a current of 48mA gives approximately 17mW at the laser output, which will result in approximately 0.85mW at each detector from the LO alone. This allows the detector to thus remain comfortably far away from its saturation point, even if returned signal is high and results in a higher incident power at the photodiode after mixing with the LO. 

\subsubsection{A3: Relative Intensity Noise}
Experiment A3 serves only for completeness, to illustrate the effectiveness of balanced detection in removing relative intensity noise at the detector output. It was found that only \SI{0.79}{\decibel} of excess RIN was present at the \SI{48}{\milli\ampere} operating point, which balanced detection then cancelled. This low level is consistent with the choice of a DFB diode (\autoref{sec:design-ComponentChoices}), whose relaxation-oscillation peak lies far above the 5--\SI{20}{\mega\hertz} measurement band, and confirms that RIN is not a significant noise source in this system; PIIN, discussed next, is. See Appendix~\ref{appendix:RINValidation} for the full results and discussion of this supplementary experiment.

\section{B: Phase-induced Intensity Noise}
\label{sec:PIINTesting}
\subsection{Experimental Goals}
As discussed in \autoref{sec:lit-noiseSources}, and elaborated in Appendix \ref{appendix:PIIN}, Phase-induced Intensity Noise (PIIN) can be a significant contributor to the total noise of the system, rising far above shot-noise - particularly when the linewidth of the laser source is not narrow, and when there is significant path-length mismatch between the LO and the transmit signal.

 The goal of Experiment B is to measure and quantify the magnitude and shape of the total noise of the system, including PIIN, in a controlled setting. There is effectively no target in this experiment, thus making all PIIN in the measurement attributable to reflections from the circulator and collimator, and independent of target. 
 
 
 \subsection{Experimental Methodology}
 For this experiment, the system is set up in full, as given by the final design in \autoref{fig:finalDesign}. The collimator is then pointed at a stationary matte-black painted surface, angled 45 degrees away from the collimator, and at a distance of 10cm from the collimator. Any reflections from the target surface will not be Doppler shifted, because it is stationary - thus, if these reflections are sent back into the collimator, they will appear at the output as additional PIIN (see Appendix \ref{appendix:PIIN}). This setup thus intends to absorb and reflect a large amount of the emitted light \textbf{away} from the system, so that the measured PIIN can be attributed to only the internal reflections in the system from the collimator and circulator, which is independent of target. 
 
 The laser is then driven with a current of 48mA (the chosen operating point for LO power), and the same oscilloscope acquisition chain and post-processing chain (Welch PSD) as in experiment A is used to obtain the PIIN power. As discussed in \cite{jiang_performance_2019}, PIIN can vary drastically depending on temperature, or small disturbances in the system's fiber optics, thus this measurement is repeated 10 times over the course of an hour to account for such variations. The average noise power as well as the spread from these measurements can then be computed. 
 

\subsection{Results and Discussion}

\subsubsection{Initial Results}
The total noise was found to be significantly above that measured in A2, owing to the excess noise introduced by PIIN. In the 5--20\,MHz band, the total noise was measured to be 22.1\,dB above the shot-noise floor. PIIN is thus the largest single noise contributor in the system, and the system is therefore no longer shot-noise limited. Experiments C and D (\autoref{sec:functionalTesting}) were first attempted using the system as is, with the PIIN-limited behaviour described above. While the system could adequately detect the velocity of a hard target, it did not have enough sensitivity to detect the velocity of a seeded air-flow, owing to the high noise floor from PIIN. It was thus decided that an attempt would be made to reduce PIIN, from the theory discussed in \autoref{sec:lit-noiseSources} and elaborated in Appendix \ref{appendix:PIIN}, and the experiment would be rerun for this improved system. 

\subsubsection{Reducing PIIN: Methodology and Results}
\label{sec:reducing_piin}
 Obtaining a narrower linewidth laser source is out of budget for this project, so the only remaining option was to better match the fibre optic path length between the LO and the internal reflections which mix with the LO to cause PIIN. This allows the LO and the reflections to be more coherent in phase at the mixer, resulting in lower PIIN (see Appendix \ref{appendix:PIIN}). FC/APC fibre patch cords of varying lengths were inserted between the 10\% splitter arm and the 50:50 mixer, to increase the LO path length to more closely match the longer path followed by the reflections. The total system noise was measured for each patch cable length using the same methodology, with 10 captures each. The results are given in \autoref{fig:psdpiinlengths} and \autoref{tab:piin_matching}, with the electronic noise floor and the total noise floor (excluding PIIN) from Experiment A2 given as reference.

As expected from the theory of Harris et al. and Jiang et al. \cite{harris_role_1998} \cite{jiang_performance_2019}, PIIN varied significantly, even between measurements with the same setup. It can also be observed that the shape of the PIIN corresponds to the $L_c \gg \Delta L$ regime described in Appendix \ref{appendix:PIIN}, as it is a flat floor and does not contain a Lorentzian peak at baseband.

It was found that increasing the patch cable length (and thus increasing the fibre path matching) significantly reduced the total noise, with the PIIN reduction loosely matching the $\Delta L^2$ dependence identified by Harris et al. \cite{harris_role_1998}. The 4m patch cable gave a remarkable 17.7dB reduction in PIIN, bringing the total noise to just 5.7dB above shot noise. While the system is still PIIN-limited, it is far closer to the ideal shot-noise limit, and this gain in sensitivity is what made the wind-speed measurements of Experiment D possible. The 4m patch cable is therefore kept in the system for all results reported in \autoref{sec:functionalTesting}.

Further reduction through path matching alone is limited, since the PIIN
arises from several reflections at different points in the transmit path,
and a single LO patch cable can match the path length of only one of these; resulting in there still being some residual PIIN. In the $L_c \gg \Delta L$ regime observed
here, the PIIN level is also however proportional to the laser linewidth $\Delta f$
(Appendix \ref{appendix:PIIN}) \cite{harris_role_1998}. Thus, replacing the
\SI{2}{MHz} DFB with a \SI{100}{kHz} source (for example an external-cavity
or narrow-linewidth fibre laser) would reduce the PIIN by approximately \SI{13}{dB} (from approximately 2.7x the shot noise level to 0.14x the shot noise level), lowering the total
noise from \SI{5.7}{dB} to about \SI{0.6}{dB} above the shot-noise limit,
an improvement of approximately \SI{5}{dB} in sensitivity. A narrower linewidth would also reduce the linewidth
broadening of the Doppler peak (Appendix \ref{appendix:PIIN}), concentrating the
return in fewer frequency bins and further improving its detectability.
